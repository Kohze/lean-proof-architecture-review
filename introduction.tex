\section{Introduction}

A formal proof can cross several mathematical languages before reaching
its conclusion. A graph becomes a Boolean matrix, a differential form
acquires a continuously bundled representation, or an estimator becomes
a program reading a particular sequence of random bits. Each crossing
raises the same question: what information must survive for the next
argument to remain valid?

We study the proved correspondences that answer this question. We call
them preservation interfaces: they specify both the relation between
representations and the information available to a later construction.
An accuracy event requires the output probability law; differentiation
requires suitable local information about a field; an equation in named
coordinates requires the coordinate maps themselves. Equivalence of
underlying objects is useful only when it supplies the relation that the
next step consumes.

The OpenAI Lean collection offers six detailed cases for this comparison
\citep{OAIRepository}. Common-base counting and graph switch chains show
how finite representations support probabilistic guarantees. The Mahler
and Crouzeix arguments show how algebraic identities, analytic estimates,
and auxiliary domains meet. Trace ideals and marked character-variety
diagrams show how an invariant or a change of coordinates can retain
enough data to reconstruct and transport a specific object. The first
four selected statements are public endpoints; the final two are support
results with their own explicit conclusions.

Three questions guide each case. What does the intermediate representation
retain? Which theorem connects it to the intended object? Where does that
theorem discharge the next step's hypotheses? The review is intended for
mathematicians reading formal developments and for formalizers choosing
interfaces. Its contribution is a source-anchored comparison, supported
by small examples that explain why a weaker correspondence can fail.

This perspective builds on established accounts of formalization.
Lean combines dependent type theory, an extensible elaborator, and a
programming language \citep{deMouraUllrich2021}; Mathlib provides a shared
mathematical vocabulary \citep{mathlib2020}.
\citet{AvigadEtAl2024Anatomy} follow a linear-algebra example through
formal proof, generalization, and connection with an existing Mathlib
theorem. \citet{CommelinTopaz2024} study abstraction boundaries for
mathematical construction and collaboration, using usable specifications
as evidence of alignment with informal mathematics. Our comparison
follows already-written preservation arguments across several settings.

Large formalizations provide further precedents. The odd-order theorem
and Kepler conjecture combined extensive libraries, proof decompositions,
and verified computation \citep{GonthierEtAl2013,HalesEtAl2017}.
Perfectoid spaces and schemes in Lean required carefully chosen objects
and interfaces \citep{Perfectoid2020,Schemes2021}. Against that background,
the interest here lies in the particular mathematical relation proved at
each transition and the operation it enables. The elementary examples
serve that explanatory purpose. Preservation of data, laws, estimates,
and local information provides a common reading framework for the cases.
