\section{Introduction}

A theorem prover checks a formal proposition, but a mathematician also wants
to know why that proposition expresses the intended argument. This question
becomes particularly interesting when a proof crosses several mathematical
languages. A graph may become a Boolean matrix, a differential form may
acquire a continuously bundled representation, or an estimator may become
a program reading a particular sequence of random bits. At each crossing,
the proof must identify the information that the next step consumes and
show that the chosen representation retains it.

Lean provides a dependent type theory, an extensible elaborator, and a
programming language \citep{deMouraUllrich2021}. Mathlib supplies a large
shared mathematical vocabulary \citep{mathlib2020}. Neither a dependent
record nor an automated arithmetic proof is unusual by itself. The useful
question is what mathematical specification a record carries, and what
argument becomes possible after that specification has been proved.
Accounts of proof anatomy and specification-driven development make this
perspective explicit \citep{AvigadEtAl2024Anatomy,CommelinTopaz2024}.

Substantial precedents also matter when assessing a new collection. The
formalizations of the odd-order theorem and the Kepler conjecture already
combined extensive libraries with large proof decompositions and verified
computation \citep{GonthierEtAl2013,HalesEtAl2017}. In Lean, perfectoid
spaces and schemes required careful choices of mathematical objects and
interfaces \citep{Perfectoid2020,Schemes2021}. Such projects show why the
presence of a difficult theorem, or a large number of declarations, is
insufficient evidence for a new formalization method. A useful review must
locate a more specific mechanism and explain its mathematical purpose.

The OpenAI collection offers an unusually broad setting in which to look
for these mechanisms \citep{OAIRepository}. We select six developments
whose sources expose a substantial transition between representations.
The first two concern finite probability and algorithms; the next two
concern analysis; the final two concern reconstruction and transport of
algebraic data. The selected statements have different roles: four are
public endpoints, while trace-ideal transport and all-seams compatibility
are support results with their own explicit conclusions. Reading the
support results on their own terms reveals useful mathematics that a list
of headline theorem titles would miss.

Three questions guide the review. What information does the intermediate
representation retain? Which theorem connects it to the intended object?
Where is that theorem used to discharge the next step's hypotheses?
For common bases, the answer includes the law of the output and the
execution cost of one fixed machine. For Mahler, it includes both equality
of form-valued fields and agreement near the integration boundary. For
character varieties, it includes named maps on graded pieces, beyond
equality of the corresponding subspaces.

The contribution is a comparative analysis of these concrete interfaces.
We give source anchors, trace the relevant definitions and hypotheses,
and separate established methods from the particular mathematical choices
made in each development. We then work out small examples that expose the
reason for the stronger specifications. These examples are elementary;
their role is explanatory. The proposed distinction between preservation
of data, laws, estimates, and local information is a reading framework,
rather than a claim that the underlying methods originate in this release.
