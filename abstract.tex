\begin{abstract}
Formal proofs often become most informative where they change
representation: a graph is encoded by a small matrix, or a random
experiment becomes a program reading specified tape bits. The mathematical
question is what must be preserved for the next proof step to remain
valid. This source-driven review examines that question in six developments
from the OpenAI Lean collection: common-base counting, graph switch chains,
the symmetric Mahler inequality, the complete Crouzeix bound, extended
trace cones, and marked character-variety constructions. We trace the
correspondences between representations and identify their downstream use.
Across the cases, the required information includes exact coordinates,
output laws, quantitative estimates, and local differential data. The
comparison explains why an output mean cannot replace a probability law,
why ordered form identities and boundary locality have different roles,
and why equal subspaces need not preserve a named-coordinate equation.
Elementary worked examples isolate these distinctions; a composed
success-budget argument connects sampling loss and tape uniformity to
an exact finite success count. Comparison with established formalization
and verified-programming literature locates the contribution in the
concrete composition of these interfaces. The review offers mathematicians
and formalizers a route from a theorem statement to the preservation
arguments that make its construction work.
\end{abstract}
